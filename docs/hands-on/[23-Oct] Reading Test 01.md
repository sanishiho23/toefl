## Passage 1 — The Great Trigonometrical Survey

> (1) In 1802 the British East India Company authorised a project of almost absurd ambition: to
> measure the entire Indian subcontinent by triangulation. The Great Trigonometrical Survey would
> eventually run for more than seventy years, employ thousands of people, and cost a substantial
> fraction of the Company's annual revenue. Its stated purpose was practical — accurate maps were
> indispensable to administration, taxation and military movement — but from the outset the
> survey was also an exercise in pure geodesy.
>
> (2) The method itself was old. By measuring one baseline with extreme care and then sighting a
> distant point from both ends, surveyors could compute the angles of a triangle and, from those,
> the length of its other two sides. Each completed triangle supplied a baseline for the next, and
> in this way a chain of triangles could be walked across a continent. What made the Indian survey
> exceptional was not the geometry but the metrology. The theodolites employed weighed over half a
> tonne and were carried in pieces by teams of porters; each measurement was repeated dozens of
> times and corrected for temperature, refraction and the sag of the measuring chains.
>
> (3) The most consequential by-product of this obsession with precision was unexpected. Working in
> the 1840s, the surveyor Andrew Waugh noticed that the positions of the great peaks along the
> northern frontier, computed from stations hundreds of kilometres away, consistently disagreed
> with the elevations estimated by field parties. Rather than average the discrepancies away, he
> had them recomputed, and by 1856 the arithmetic had settled on a single summit in the Nepal
> Himalaya whose computed height exceeded 29,000 feet. It had no local name that Waugh could
> establish; he proposed naming it after his predecessor, Sir George Everest, over the objections
> of those who argued that a mountain should carry a name pronounceable by the people who lived
> beside it.
>
> (4) The survey also settled a question that had occupied physicists since Newton. Because the
> Himalayan massif is enormous, its gravitational attraction was expected to deflect a plumb line
> from the vertical, and surveyors duly found discrepancies between astronomical and geodetic
> latitude. But the observed deflection was consistently about a third of what the visible
> mountains implied. The explanation, supplied eventually by J. H. Pratt and George Airy, was that
> the crust beneath the mountains is less dense than the crust beneath the plains — that mountains
> have roots. The idea of isostasy, that the crust floats in gravitational balance, was born
> largely from a mapping project.

## Passage 2 — Leafcutter Ants

> (1) The mushroom gardens of leafcutter ants are among the most sophisticated agricultural systems
> outside human farming, and they have been evolving for roughly fifty million years. A mature
> colony of *Atta cephalotes* may contain five million workers and move several hundred kilograms
> of vegetation a year into underground chambers where they cultivate a fungus that is, in effect,
> their only food. The comparison with agriculture is not loose: these ants sow, weed, fertilise
> and harvest, and they have been doing so since long before any primate planted anything.
>
> (2) The system rests on a partnership of unusual intimacy. The fungus, a member of the family
> Agaricaceae, has been propagated clonally by ants for so long that it no longer survives
> independently in the wild. In exchange for shelter, substrate and defence, it converts leaf
> tissue — which the ants cannot digest — into specialised structures rich in glycogen that the
> larvae and adults can eat. Workers maintain the garden with a precision that still surprises
> researchers: they remove alien fungal strains, apply antibiotic secretions produced by bacteria
> living on their own cuticles, and adjust the pH of the substrate.
>
> (3) What makes the arrangement fragile is its narrow genetic base. Because the crop is
> essentially a single clone, it lacks the variation that a sexually reproducing population would
> deploy against a pathogen. The colonies' principal answer is chemical and social rather than
> genetic. In addition to the antibiotics, workers quarantine contaminated sections, and — the
> discovery that drew most attention — they engage in a form of waste management in which older
> foragers, whose remaining life expectancy is short, are barred from the chambers where the
> youngest and most valuable workers tend the fungus.
>
> (4) The agricultural analogy has one instructive limit. Human agriculture became possible
> because plants could be bred; ant agriculture became possible because a fungus could be
> defended. Neither partner in the ant system is domesticated in the sense that wheat is — the
> fungus has not been bred for yield, and the ants have not lost the ability to live otherwise
> should the garden fail. The stability of the system therefore rests not on genetic control but
> on continuous, energetically expensive maintenance. When a colony stops paying that cost — when
> the foragers die, or the queen fails — the garden collapses within days.
