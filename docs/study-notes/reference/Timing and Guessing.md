# Timing & Guessing

> Most people who miss their target on TOEFL ITP don't run out of *knowledge*.
> They run out of *minutes*. This file is about the clock.

---

## 1. The pace you must hold

| Section | Questions | Time | Per question | Trains as |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Listening | 50 | 35 min | **fixed by the audio** | you cannot control it — you can only not fall behind |
| Structure | 40 | 25 min | **37 s** | the tightest section on the test |
| Reading | 50 | 55 min | **66 s** | ~11 min per passage |
| **Total** | **140** | **115 min** | | |

---

## 2. Checkpoints — write these on your hand

### Structure (25 min)
- **12:30 remaining** → you must be at question 20.
- **06:00 remaining** → you must be at question 30.
- Below pace at either checkpoint → guess the rest of the current block and jump forward.

### Reading (55 min)
- **30:00 remaining** → past question 25.
- **15:00 remaining** → past question 38.
- **05:00 remaining** → everything bubbled, even if guessed.

---

## 3. The 40-second rule

> **If you haven't chosen an answer in 40 seconds, you don't know it. Guess and move.**

Why it works: the question you're stuck on is worth 1 point. The three questions you could have
answered in that time are worth 3. And you can come back if time remains — but usually you won't,
and that's fine.

**The mechanics of a good guess:**
1. Eliminate the one option you're most sure is wrong.
2. Pick from the remaining three.
3. Mark the question in your test book with a circled number.
4. **Do not spend another second.**

---

## 4. How to guess well (when you have zero information)

| Section | Reliable bias |
|---|---|
| Listening | **Avoid extreme options.** *furious, impossible, never, always* are wrong ~80% of the time. Prefer moderate paraphrases. |
| Structure | If two options are the same word in different forms, the answer is usually one of them. For error-finding, **word form** and **preposition** are the most likely culprits. |
| Reading | For *main idea*, eliminate the too-broad and too-narrow options first — the middle one is usually right. For *inference*, the mildest claim wins. |
| All | An option that repeats words from the passage verbatim is often a **distractor**. The right answer is usually a paraphrase. |

---

## 5. Listening: the pace you actually control

You can't speed up the audio, but you *can* control two things:

1. **Answer transfer.** Fill bubbles during the stated interval. Don't hoard answers to transfer
   at the end — proctors stop you, and you lose them all.
2. **Recovery time.** The moment a dialogue ends, your brain must be empty for the next one.
   Practise this deliberately: in every drill, after a question you got wrong, say out loud
   *"gone"* and look at the next item. That reflex is worth 5 points.

---

## 6. Reading: the 11-minute passage budget

| Min | Task |
|---:|---|
| 0:00–3:30 | Read the passage once, at a steady pace. Underline topic sentences. |
| 3:30–9:00 | Answer in [type order](<../reading/[19-Oct] R01 - Question Types.md>): vocab → detail → NOT/EXCEPT → reference → inference → main idea → tone → insert |
| 9:00–11:00 | Bubble check + the two questions you skipped |

If a passage is unusually hard, **don't** spend 15 minutes on it. Cap at 13, guess the rest, and
buy your points on an easier passage later in the section. Passages are not ordered by difficulty.

---

## 7. Practise the clock, not just the content

Every drill in this course is timed for a reason. Specifically:

- **Structure sprints** (Week 4, D31) run at 22 min instead of 25 — deliberately faster than
  the exam, so 25 minutes feels slow.
- **Reading sets** run at 26 min instead of 28.
- **Mocks** run at exactly 115 min, straight through, no breaks except the real one.

> **Never do an untimed drill after Week 1.** Untimed practice measures something the exam
> does not measure, and it will make you overconfident.
