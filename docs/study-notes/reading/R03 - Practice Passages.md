## Passage 1 — Urban Heat Islands

> (1) Anyone who has stepped off a train in a city centre on a summer evening and then returned
> to the countryside an hour later has experienced a measurable meteorological phenomenon: the
> urban heat island. First documented systematically by Luke Howard in London in the eighteen-twenties,
> the effect refers to the fact that built-up areas are consistently warmer than the rural land
> surrounding them, sometimes by as much as ten degrees Celsius at night.
>
> (2) The causes are straightforward, though they operate together rather than in isolation.
> The most obvious is the thermal property of the materials themselves. Asphalt, concrete and
> brick absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly after sunset, whereas soil
> and vegetation release heat comparatively quickly. A second factor is geometry. Tall buildings
> create what climatologists call urban canyons, which trap long-wave radiation between their
> surfaces and reduce the wind speeds that would otherwise carry warm air away. A third is the
> absence of vegetation: plants cool their surroundings through evapotranspiration, a process
> unavailable where the ground has been sealed. And a fourth is anthropogenic heat — the waste
> energy from vehicles, air-conditioning units and industrial processes, which in dense districts
> can rival the solar input.
>
> (3) The consequences are not evenly distributed. Because the effect is strongest at night and
> during heat waves, it raises the frequency of dangerously hot periods rather than merely raising
> average temperatures. Public health researchers have consequently recorded higher rates of
> heat-related illness among residents of low-income neighbourhoods, which typically have less
> tree cover and a greater proportion of heat-absorbing surfaces. The phenomenon therefore has
> a dimension that is as much social as meteorological.
>
> (4) Municipal responses have varied in ambition. The cheapest and most frequently adopted is
> the installation of cool roofs — surfaces finished with reflective coatings that reject rather
> than absorb short-wave radiation. More ambitious programmes have pursued urban forestry,
> although mature canopy cover takes decades to establish. A third approach, still experimental
> at scale, involves altering the albedo of paving materials across entire districts. What the
> evidence increasingly suggests is that no single intervention matches the effectiveness of
> several applied together.

## Passage 2 — The Mechanical Clock and the Measurement of Time

> (1) Before the fourteenth century, the hours in Europe were not fixed units at all. They were
> canonical hours — prayer times announced by bells — and, being tied to the rising and setting
> of the sun, they varied in length with the seasons. A summer "hour" was longer than a winter
> one, and the very notion of being late, in our sense, barely existed. Time was something that
> happened; it was not something one consulted.
>
> (2) The mechanical clock changed this, though not as quickly or as completely as is often
> assumed. The earliest public clocks were monumental objects, installed in towers and financed
> by merchants and guilds rather than by churches. Crucially, they struck the hours but typically
> possessed no minute hand, and their accuracy was poor — a quarter of an hour's drift per day
> was unremarkable. What they did establish was not precision but **convention**: the agreement
> that an hour is the same length regardless of the season, and that the same hour is shared by
> everyone in the town.
>
> (3) The consequences were gradual but far-reaching. Because work could now be measured, wages
> could be calculated by the hour, and the distinction between time spent and work completed
> became a source of contention that persists in labour relations to this day. Municipal authorities
> discovered that a public clock permitted the coordination of markets, gate closings and curfews
> with a precision previously impossible. By the sixteenth century, the clock had begun to appear
> in private households, and with it came a new interior sense — that one's own day was a resource
> that could be spent well or badly.
>
> (4) Historians disagree about how to weigh this transformation. One tradition, associated with
> the French historian Jacques Le Goff, treats the shift from "church time" to "merchant time" as
> a decisive break, the moment when time became a commodity. A more recent scholarship cautions
> against overstating the case: peasants continued to organise their year around the agricultural
> calendar for centuries, and even in towns, the clock's authority was limited by the simple fact
> that most people could not see it. The pendulum clock of sixteen fifty-seven, which reduced daily
> error to a matter of seconds, is arguably a better candidate for the moment when precision
> genuinely entered ordinary life.

### Explanations worth reading

- **Q3** — a NOT/EXCEPT item. Paragraph 2 says vegetation is *absent*; evapotranspiration therefore
  cools rural areas, not urban ones. The other three are stated causes.
- **Q7** — *rival* here means "be comparable to", i.e. **equal**, not "exceed".
- **Q8** — the author's adjectives (*cheapest, more ambitious, still experimental*) are evaluative
  but measured → **analytical**, not dismissive or enthusiastic.
- **Q12** — canonical hours = the prayer hours of the church → **B**. Note this is a vocabulary
  item where the "church" sense is required, not the "fixed unit" sense.
- **Q20** — *"arguably a better candidate for the moment when precision genuinely entered ordinary
  life"* → the author is offering an alternative dating, i.e. **B**.
