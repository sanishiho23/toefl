## Passage 3 — Coral Reefs and Thermal Stress

> (1) Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor, yet they shelter roughly a
> quarter of all marine species. This disproportion arises from a partnership. Reef-building
> corals house microscopic algae, called zooxanthellae, within their tissues. The algae use
> sunlight to produce sugars, much of which they pass to the coral, and in return the coral
> supplies shelter and the nutrients the algae need. The arrangement is so efficient that reefs
> flourish in tropical waters that are otherwise poor in nutrients.
>
> (2) The partnership is, however, fragile. When water temperatures rise even one or two degrees
> above the seasonal maximum for several weeks, the algae begin to produce compounds that harm
> the coral, which responds by expelling them. Because the algae supply both food and colour, the
> coral turns pale, a condition known as bleaching. A bleached coral is not dead, but it is
> starving, and unless temperatures fall within a few weeks, it will usually die.
>
> (3) Bleaching events were once rare, local occurrences. Since the 1980s, however, they have
> become more frequent and widespread, with mass events now affecting reefs across entire ocean
> basins. Some researchers have noted that reefs previously exposed to moderate warming sometimes
> withstand later heat better than those that have not, suggesting a capacity for acclimatization.
> Others caution that this resilience has limits: recovery takes years, and if events recur faster
> than corals can regrow, the reef cannot rebuild.
>
> (4) Conservation efforts therefore focus on two fronts. The first is reducing local pressures,
> such as pollution and overfishing, which leave reefs less able to recover. The second, which no
> local policy can address, is the warming of the oceans themselves. Many scientists contend that
> without substantial cuts in greenhouse gas emissions, protecting individual reefs will only delay,
> not prevent, their decline.

## Passage 4 — Sleep and Memory

> (1) For most of the twentieth century, sleep was regarded as a passive state, a nightly shutdown
> during which the brain simply rested. Beginning in the 1950s, however, recordings of brain
> activity showed that the sleeping brain is anything but idle. It cycles through distinct stages,
> including rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, during which neural activity resembles that of waking.
>
> (2) One of the most robust findings to emerge from this research concerns memory. In a typical
> experiment, participants learn a list of word pairs or a motor skill, such as a sequence of
> finger movements, and are tested after a delay. Those who sleep during the delay consistently
> perform better than those who spend the same interval awake. The difference cannot be attributed
> merely to the absence of interference from new experiences, since sleep improves performance even
> on skills that participants had not fully mastered before sleeping.
>
> (3) Researchers propose that during deep, slow-wave sleep the brain replays recently formed
> memories, gradually transferring them from the hippocampus, which stores them temporarily, to
> the cortex, where they are integrated with existing knowledge. REM sleep, by contrast, appears
> to support the linking of unrelated ideas, which may explain why people sometimes wake with a
> solution to a problem that eluded them the previous evening.
>
> (4) These findings carry practical implications. Students who sacrifice sleep to study through
> the night may be undermining the very process that would consolidate what they have learned.
> Yet the evidence should not be overstated. Most experiments involve simple tasks in laboratory
> conditions, and it remains unclear how far the results extend to the complex, long-term learning
> that characterises education.

### Explanations worth reading

- **Q24 (NOT/EXCEPT)** — three options are stated in paragraph 2. "Within hours" is never said: the text says the coral is *not dead* and dies only if the heat lasts *a few weeks*.
- **Q28 / Q29 / Q40 (tone)** — the author qualifies claims (*real but limited*, *should not be overstated*). Extreme options (*unlimited*, *dismissive*, *hostile*) are almost never right.
- **Q35 (NOT mentioned)** — brain recordings appear in paragraph 1, not in the paragraph 2 experiment description.
