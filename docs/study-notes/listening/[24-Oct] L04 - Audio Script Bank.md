# L04 — Audio Script Bank

**How to use:** do **not** read these first. Feed the script to a text-to-speech app
(or have someone read it aloud at ~150 wpm), play it **once**, take notes, answer the
questions, and only then read the text. That is the only way this file trains anything.

Scripts are written in TOEFL ITP register: North American campus English, academic content.

---

## Script 1 — Part B style: Office hours (campus conversation)

> **W:** Professor Hahn, do you have a minute? I'm still not clear on the format for the
> midterm.
> **M:** Of course, Nadia. Come in. What's confusing you?
> **W:** Well, the syllabus says the exam covers chapters four through nine, but in class on
> Tuesday you mentioned that chapter ten would also be included. I want to make sure I study
> the right material.
> **M:** Ah, you're right — I did say that, and I should have corrected it. Chapter ten is
> material for the final, not the midterm. I'll post an announcement on the course page today.
> Stick with four through nine.
> **W:** Great, that saves me a lot of time. One more thing — will the exam be entirely
> multiple choice, or will there be short-answer questions as well?
> **M:** Roughly seventy per cent multiple choice, and the remaining thirty per cent will be
> short definitions of key terms. I'd recommend memorising the glossary on page 212.
> **W:** That's very helpful. And how long will we have?
> **M:** The class period — eighty minutes. But in my experience most students finish in about
> an hour, so don't rush.
> **W:** Thank you, Professor. I feel much better about it now.

**Questions**
1. What is the main topic of the conversation?
   (A) A grade the student received  (B) The format and content of the midterm
   (C) A chapter the professor has not finished writing  (D) The date of the final exam
2. What correction does the professor make?
   (A) Chapter ten will not be on the midterm.  (B) The exam is next Tuesday.
   (C) Chapters four through nine have been removed.  (D) The syllabus is unavailable online.
3. What does the professor advise the student to do?
   (A) Study chapters four through ten  (B) Memorise the glossary on page 212
   (C) Write a practice essay  (D) Drop the course
4. How much time will students have for the exam?
   (A) Sixty minutes  (B) Eighty minutes  (C) Ninety minutes  (D) One hundred minutes

**Answers:** 1-B · 2-A · 3-B · 4-B
*Note: Q2 is a "correction" item — professors correcting themselves is a classic Part B target.*

## Script 2 — Part C style: Biology lecture

> Today I'd like to continue our discussion of animal communication, and specifically to look
> at one of the most remarkable systems known to science: the honeybee's waggle dance.
>
> When a foraging bee returns to the hive having found a rich source of nectar, she does not
> simply lead the others there. Instead she performs a figure-eight dance on the vertical face
> of the comb. There are two components to this dance, and both carry information.
>
> First, the angle. The straight portion of the dance — the "waggle run" — is performed at a
> particular angle relative to vertical. That angle is the same as the angle between the food
> source and the sun. In other words, the bee uses gravity as a stand-in for the sun's position.
>
> Second, duration. The longer she waggles, the farther away the food is. Roughly speaking, one
> second of waggling corresponds to about a kilometre. The recruits then fly out and locate the
> source using the direction and distance they have just been told.
>
> What makes this so astonishing is that it is genuinely symbolic. The bee is reporting
> something that is not present — a location in space and time — using a code that other bees
> can read. Until the work of Karl von Frisch in the nineteen-forties, most researchers assumed
> insects were incapable of anything of the kind.
>
> I should add one caveat. Recent studies suggest the system is not perfectly precise; recruits
> often have to search a little once they arrive. But the basic finding is not in dispute.

**Questions**
1. What is the main topic of the lecture?
   (A) How bees navigate using the sun  (B) The honeybee's waggle dance as a communication system
   (C) The history of entomology  (D) How nectar is converted into honey
2. According to the lecture, what does the angle of the waggle run indicate?
   (A) The distance to the food  (B) The quality of the nectar
   (C) The direction of the food relative to the sun  (D) The altitude of the hive
3. What does the duration of the waggle communicate?
   (A) Distance to the food  (B) The number of recruits needed
   (C) The time of day  (D) The type of flower
4. What does the professor say about recent research?
   (A) It has completely disproved von Frisch's work.
   (B) It shows the system is not entirely precise.
   (C) It proves bees cannot communicate direction.
   (D) It was conducted before the nineteen-forties.
5. What is the professor's attitude toward the waggle dance?
   (A) Dismissive  (B) Impressed  (C) Confused  (D) Indifferent

**Answers:** 1-B · 2-C · 3-A · 4-B · 5-B
*Note: Q5 is an attitude item — "remarkable", "astonishing", "genuinely symbolic" all point to B.*

## Script 3 — Part C style: Geology lecture

> Before we begin, let me make sure everyone has the handout — no? Then I'll wait a moment
> while copies go around. Right. Today we're moving from igneous to metamorphic rocks, and I
> want to focus on one particular example: slate.
>
> Slate begins life as shale, a sedimentary rock made of compressed clay and silt. When shale
> is buried and subjected to heat and directed pressure — what we call differential stress —
> something important happens to its internal structure. The clay minerals begin to recrystallise,
> and critically, they recrystallise with their flat plates aligned perpendicular to the
> direction of the pressure.
>
> This alignment is what geologists call foliation, and it has a practical consequence that
> anyone who has handled a piece of slate will recognise: the rock splits into thin, flat sheets
> along parallel planes. This property is called cleavage, and it is why slate has been used for
> roofing and blackboards for centuries.
>
> Now, one thing I want to emphasise: slate is a low-grade metamorphic rock. If you keep
> increasing the temperature and pressure, foliation becomes more pronounced and you progress
> through a sequence — slate to phyllite, then schist, and finally gneiss, which is banded rather
> than simply platy. So the rock type tells you something about the intensity of the conditions
> it formed under. That's a powerful diagnostic tool when you're mapping a region.

**Questions**
1. What is the lecture mainly about?
   (A) How igneous rocks form  (B) The formation and properties of slate
   (C) The use of shale in construction  (D) Methods for dating rocks
2. What is foliation?
   (A) A type of clay mineral  (B) The alignment of recrystallised minerals
   (C) The chemical composition of shale  (D) A method of measuring temperature
3. Why has slate been used for roofing?
   (A) It is very light.  (B) It splits into flat sheets.
   (C) It is found everywhere.  (D) It resists fire.
4. According to the professor, what does gneiss indicate?
   (A) Low-grade metamorphism  (B) Higher-grade metamorphism than schist
   (C) A sedimentary origin  (D) A lack of pressure
5. What does the professor do at the beginning of the lecture?
   (A) Returns an assignment  (B) Distributes a handout
   (C) Announces an exam  (D) Reviews yesterday's material

**Answers:** 1-B · 2-B · 3-B · 4-B · 5-B

## Script 4 — Part A style: 10 short dialogues

1. **W:** Did you get the lab results back yet?
   **M:** Are you kidding? The technician said it'd be another week.
   → **He is a long way from getting them.**

2. **M:** Do you want to grab lunch before the seminar?
   **W:** I'd love to, but I've got to run — my advisor is expecting me at noon.
   → **She declines because she has no time.**

3. **W:** Was the lecture any good?
   **M:** I couldn't agree more with the professor's argument.
   → **He strongly agrees with it.** *(Careful: this idiom means agreement, not "I attended".)*

4. **M:** Should I take Professor Reyes's course next term?
   **W:** If I were you, I'd check the workload first. She's known for assigning a lot.
   → **She suggests investigating before deciding.**

5. **W:** How was the exam?
   **M:** Let's just say I'll be studying harder next time.
   → **It went badly.**

6. **M:** I can't find my calculator anywhere.
   **W:** You borrowed mine last week, remember?
   → **She is reminding him.**

7. **W:** The dean approved our budget request.
   **M:** You're kidding! I thought it was out of the question.
   → **He is surprised; he assumed it was impossible.**

8. **M:** Did everyone hand in the assignment?
   **W:** All but two people.
   → **Two people did not.**

9. **W:** This reading is brutal.
   **M:** Tell me about it. I've read the same paragraph four times.
   → **He agrees it is difficult.**

10. **M:** Are you coming to the field trip on Saturday?
    **W:** I wouldn't miss it for the world.
    → **She definitely plans to go.**

## Script 5 — Part C style: Art history lecture

> I want to spend today's session on a movement that is often misunderstood: the Arts and Crafts
> movement in late nineteenth-century Britain.
>
> The conventional story is that it was a reaction against industrialisation, and that is
> broadly true. William Morris and his followers were horrified by the quality of mass-produced
> goods — furniture that fell apart, wallpaper printed with careless patterns. Their answer was
> to return to handcraft, to the medieval guild model, and to the idea that the person who
> designs an object should also make it.
>
> But here's the irony, and it's the point I want you to take away. The movement was anti-industrial
> in its rhetoric and yet thoroughly dependent on industry in practice. Morris's own firm sold its
> goods through retail showrooms, used machine-made pigments, and marketed to precisely the
> wealthy middle class whose purchasing power had been created by the factories he criticised.
> The hand-woven aesthetic was, in a real sense, a luxury product.
>
> So was the movement a failure? I'd argue not — but the honest answer is that it succeeded in a
> different way than it intended. Its direct influence on manufacturing was small. Its influence
> on design education, on architecture, and on how we think about the relationship between
> labour and objects, was enormous.

**Questions**
1. What is the main topic? (A) Industrialisation in Britain (B) The Arts and Crafts movement
   (C) Medieval guilds (D) Retail showrooms
2. What was the movement reacting against? (A) Poor-quality mass production (B) Handcraft
   (C) Medieval art (D) Design education
3. What irony does the professor point out? (A) Morris disliked factories but relied on them economically
   (B) The movement was run by factory owners (C) Handcraft was cheaper than machines
   (D) Morris never sold anything
4. According to the professor, where was the movement's real influence?
   (A) Manufacturing volume (B) Design education and architecture (C) Factory legislation (D) Textile exports
5. What is the professor's overall judgment? (A) It failed completely (B) It succeeded differently than intended
   (C) It was purely rhetorical (D) It changed nothing

**Answers:** 1-B · 2-A · 3-A · 4-B · 5-B
