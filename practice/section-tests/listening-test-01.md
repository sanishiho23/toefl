## Part B — One longer conversation (questions 9–12)

> **W:** Hi, I'm here about the summer research position. I emailed Professor Hahn last week.
> **M:** Yes, Nadia — I'm his lab manager. Come in. He's in a meeting, but I can go over the
> practical details with you.
> **W:** Great. I wasn't sure whether the position was still open.
> **M:** It is, but the deadline is the fifteenth, so you have about ten days. There are two things
> we need. First, a one-page statement describing why you're interested in the work — specifically
> which of our three projects you'd want to join. Second, an unofficial transcript. We don't need
> letters of recommendation at this stage.
> **W:** Three projects? The website only mentioned two.
> **M:** That's right, we added a third about a month ago — it's on soil microbial communities,
> which is a bit different from the other two. I'd suggest reading the abstracts on the lab page
> before you write the statement. Applicants who name a specific project and explain why are taken
> noticeably more seriously than those who just say they're interested in the lab.
> **W:** That's really helpful. And how long does the position run?
> **M:** Ten weeks, starting the first week of June. It's full-time — thirty-five hours — and the
> stipend is four thousand dollars. Most students find that it's enough to cover rent but not
> much else, so plan accordingly.
> **W:** Understood. One more thing — is it open to second-year students? I'm not a junior yet.
> **M:** Officially it says junior or senior, but last year we took two sophomores. If the rest of
> your application is strong, it's worth applying anyway.

## Part C — Talk 1 (questions 13–16)

> Today I want to introduce a distinction that will come up repeatedly in this course, and which
> students reliably find confusing at first: the difference between weather and climate.
>
> Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time. It is what you experience
> when you step outside — temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation. It is chaotic in the technical
> sense: small changes in initial conditions produce large changes in outcome, which is why
> forecasts degrade sharply beyond about ten days.
>
> Climate is different in kind, not merely in scale. Climate is the statistics of weather over a
> long period — conventionally thirty years — calculated for a region. It includes averages, but
> it also includes the variability: the range, the extremes, the frequency of unusual events. So
> climate is not "weather averaged out"; it is the full distribution of weather outcomes.
>
> Here is the consequence that matters. Because climate describes a distribution, a single cold day
> tells you nothing about climate, and neither does a single warm year. What climate science can
> legitimately claim is not that any particular storm was caused by long-term warming, but that the
> *distribution* has shifted — that certain kinds of events have become more probable than they
> were. This is why attribution studies are phrased in terms of likelihood rather than certainty,
> and why they are so often misreported.
>
> One more point, and then I'll stop. The thirty-year convention is not arbitrary physics; it's a
> practical compromise. Shorter windows are too noisy. Longer ones conceal trends, because by the
> time a sixty-year average responds, the climate it describes no longer exists.

## Part C — Talk 2 (questions 17–20)

> I'd like to spend this session on a figure who is usually mentioned in one sentence and then
> forgotten: Ada Lovelace.
>
> The standard account is that she wrote the first computer program, and that is roughly true but
> misleading. What Lovelace actually produced, in eighteen forty-three, was a translation of an
> Italian engineer's paper on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine — and, more importantly, a set of
> notes appended to that translation that ran three times longer than the original paper.
>
> It's in those notes that the interesting material sits. Babbage's own writing on the Engine is
> essentially about calculation: the machine manipulates numbers. Lovelace saw something further.
> She observed that if the machine could operate on symbols according to rules, and if numbers could
> stand for anything — musical pitches, letters, positions — then the Engine was not limited to
> arithmetic at all. She wrote that it might "compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of
> any degree of complexity." That is the conceptual leap from calculator to computer, and she made
> it a century before anyone could build the thing.
>
> There's a second point I want to make, which is about credit. In the notes she published in
> eighteen forty-three, Lovelace is explicit that the ideas are Babbage's and that she is only
> elaborating them. Modern scholarship suggests this was in part strategic: a woman asserting
> independent mathematical authority in eighteen forties England faced consequences that a
> commentator did not. Her correspondence with Babbage shows her pressing him on technical points
> with a confidence the published notes conceal.
>
> So the honest summary is awkward in a way that biographies dislike. She was neither the sole
> inventor the popular press describes, nor the mere amanuensis that nineteenth-century
> obituaries implied. She was a collaborator and, on the question of what the machine meant, the
> more perceptive of the two.

### Explanations for the five that most people miss

- **Q3** — *"I couldn't agree more"* means agreement, not attendance. The trap option is (B).
- **Q8** — *"All but two"* = everyone except two → **two did not submit**. Opposite of (A).
- **Q10** — *"the fifteenth"* is stated explicitly; *ten weeks* and *the first week of June*
  describe the position, not the deadline.
- **Q15** — the speaker explicitly denies (A). The correct claim is about the *distribution*.
- **Q20** — *"in part strategic"* → (D). Note it is **not** (C): modern scholarship supports the
  substance of her contribution while reinterpreting her self-presentation.
